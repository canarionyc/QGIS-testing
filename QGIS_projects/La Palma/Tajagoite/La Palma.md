# La Palma: Territorial Transformation & Volcanic Catalyst - Project Summary

**Date:** April 19, 2026  
**Author:** Tomás González Llarena  
**Subject:** Analysis of Socioeconomic and Territorial Impact of the 2021 Tajogaite Eruption

---

## 1. Executive Summary
This document summarizes the analytical findings regarding the 2021 Tajogaite eruption as a **spatial fix** and catalyst for territorial change on the island of La Palma. It contrasts the traditional agricultural model (the "Agricultural Shield") with the post-COVID push for intensive, high-end tourist development supported by the *Ley de Islas Verdes*.

## 2. The Macroeconomic Timeline
The current territorial strategy is not a spontaneous reaction to the volcano but the revival of a long-standing agenda:
* **2007:** Drafting of the PTETLPA (Special Territorial Plan for Tourist Planning).
* **2008:** The global financial crisis "kills" the implementation of these plans.
* **2019-2021:** The COVID-19 pandemic pauses the sector but eventually leads to a "financialization" of tourism.
* **2021:** The eruption provides a legal and physical "clean slate" to bypass previous frictions.

## 3. Comparative Context: The Green Islands Law (Ley 3/2016)
The transformation of La Palma is mirrored in its sister islands, El Hierro and La Gomera, even without volcanic activity. The common denominator is the **Ley de Islas Verdes**, which allows for the liberalization of rural soil (*suelo rústico*).
* **El Hierro:** Massive bed-capacity increases in La Frontera on agricultural land.
* **La Gomera:** Shift toward luxury nature tourism and boutique ruralism.

## 4. Destruction of the Agricultural Shield
Historically, the banana sector (supported by POSEI funds) prevented the encroachment of tourism into the Aridane Valley. The volcano destroyed this shield by:
1.  **Burying the land:** Creating a sterile malpaís that requires massive capital (sorribas) to recover.
2.  **Severing infrastructure:** Destroying irrigation networks.
3.  **Water Scarcity:** Alternative crops (avocados, papayas) are too water-intensive for the post-disaster economy.

## 5. The "Ghost Cadastre" and Ownership
The land ownership of the new territory remains in a legal limbo:
* **Private Owners:** Still own the "lava crust" over their original plots but are often incentivized to cede it to the administration.
* **The State:** Automatically owns the new *fajanas* (lava deltas) formed in the sea.
* **The Developers:** Positioned to consolidate these fragmented plots for large-scale projects like golf courses and "eco-resorts."

## 6. The New Tourism Aesthetic: "The Architecture of Disruption"
The shift is also aesthetic, as seen in the "otherworldly" design trends like the **Silken Saaj Maar** (Puerto de la Cruz):
* **Parametric Design:** "Punched" or irregular facades that signal a break from traditional heritage.
* **Cantilevers:** Private luxury spaces overhanging public promenades.
* **Instagrammability:** Designing for visual consumption rather than community integration.

## 7. Geothermal Potential
The eruption has unlocked ~€48M in European funding for deep geothermal exploration. This represents a rare opportunity for La Palma to achieve **energy independence** and shift away from its 90% diesel dependency.

## 8. Conclusion and Social Tension
The structural shift in tourism is meeting fierce local resistance. The "Canarias tiene un límite" movement and anti-tourist graffiti (e.g., in Tenerife) reflect a growing sense of **territorial theft**. The volcano is being used as a catalyst, but the social cost remains a primary concern for the island's future.
